# Translation as an Act of Hospitality

Every serious translator inhabits a paradox that classical rhetoric never quite resolved: fidelity to the source sentence frequently produces estrangement in the host tongue, whereas effortless fluency risks erasing the very foreignness that justified the crossing. Paul Ricoeur described this tension as *linguistic hospitality*—the willingness to invite another syntax, another historical memory, into one's own dwelling without forcing it to surrender its accent at the threshold.

## The Residue That Refuses Equivalence

Consider how poorly dictionary equivalence accounts for cadence, kinship terms, or seasonal ecology. When a Sangam poet names *neydal*—the coastal landscape of longing at dusk—no single English noun carries both the salt marsh and the emotional register of patient separation. A conscientious translation cannot pretend the gap does not exist; instead, it bends the receiving language slightly until the reader senses the contour of an unfamiliar shore.

In this light, translation is neither transparent glass nor betrayal. It is an ethical discipline of attentive listening—one that reminds every monolingual culture that its own grammar is only one local dialect of human meaning.
