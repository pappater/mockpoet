# How Clocks Changed the Human Sense of Patience

Before mechanical escapements began ticking inside medieval bell towers, time was largely an atmospheric event. People measured the day by shadow length, hunger, candle wax, and the angle of light across a courtyard wall. Waiting for bread to rise or for a ship to clear the breakwater required a porous kind of patience—an acceptance that things finished when their own internal chemistry was ready.

## From Weather to Arithmetic

The town clock replaced organic readiness with uniform slices. Once an hour was divided into sixty identical minutes—and later into seconds visible on a pocket dial—waiting stopped feeling like rest and began feeling like friction. If a train is due at 4:12 and arrives at 4:19, seven minutes acquire an emotional weight out of all proportion to their length.

Yet the clock did not only manufacture impatience; it also made coordination possible on a vast scale. Symphonies, rail networks, and quiet appointments between friends owe their existence to shared seconds. The challenge of modern life is not to smash the watch, but to remember which human experiences—grief, friendship, deep reading—refuse to obey its subdivisions.
