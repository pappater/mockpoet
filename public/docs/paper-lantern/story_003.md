# Postcards from an Unbuilt Station

Every Sunday after lunch, Subhash took the 2:15 local train two stops past his own neighborhood just to sit on the bench at Halcyon Halt, a concrete platform that had been poured in 1998 for a commuter line extension that never received its signal box.

No express trains stopped there. Weeds threaded neatly through the expansion joints, and the timetable board held only a faded blueprint behind cracked acrylic. Yet the bench faced west across a salt marsh, and on clear afternoons the reeds turned the color of toasted coriander while commuter trains hurtled past without slowing, rattling the empty tin awning for three seconds before restoring the silence.

His daughter Meera discovered his habit only after he broke his wrist slipping on wet stairs in January and asked her to fetch his leather satchel from the hall closet. Inside, wrapped in a cotton handkerchief, were fourteen postcards addressed to *S. Chatterjee, Platform 2, Halcyon Halt*—all written in his own careful fountain-pen hand, stamped with cancelled domestic postage he had mailed to himself from post offices across the city whenever he traveled for audits.

"Baba," she said, setting the kettle on the stove, "why did you write yourself postcards to a station that doesn't have a mailbox?"

Subhash looked down at his plaster cast, then at the steam rising from the spout. "Because at a real station," he said softly, "everyone is either arriving late or leaving too soon. I wanted one place in my week where nothing was expected to happen on schedule."

The following Sunday, though his wrist still ached in the damp air, Meera drove him down the gravel service lane beside the marsh. She brought two thermoses of cardamom tea and a folding camp chair. For an hour neither of them checked a clock. When the 3:40 express roared past, shaking the gravel beneath their shoes, Meera reached into her coat pocket and handed him an unsealed envelope.

Inside was a fresh postcard showing the old stone bridge over the river. On the back, she had written six words: *The 3:40 is still on time.*

Subhash smiled, tucked the card inside his coat beside the others, and watched the reeds bend in the salt wind—grateful, for the first time in years, that someone else knew the way to the platform.
