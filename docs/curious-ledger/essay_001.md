# The Quiet Ethics of Borrowing a Book

When someone hands you a book from their own shelf, they are rarely giving you mere paper and glue. They are handing you a season of their life—often marked by a receipt tucked into chapter four, a faint coffee ring on the copyright page, or a corner folded down on a rainy Tuesday years ago.

Borrowing a book comes with an unwritten etiquette that has nothing to do with library late fees. First, you keep the jacket intact. Second, you never crack the spine flat against a kitchen table while eating toast. Third, and most importantly, you actually read it—or at least try honestly before returning it with a real sentence about why it stayed on your nightstand.

There is a small kind of trust in lending stories. Unlike borrowing a ladder or a wrench, you do not return a book unchanged; you return it carrying a conversation neither person has spoken aloud yet. When you hand it back across a café table and say, "I kept thinking about that scene on the train," the book becomes a bridge between two private afternoons.
