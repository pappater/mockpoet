# Chapter 31: The Grove Felled

By noon the rain had thinned to a cold mist that clung to the silver bark of the birch grove like smoke from an unextinguished hearth. Dmitri Ivanovich stood at the edge of the clearing with his ledger tucked beneath his arm—not his father’s worn leather volume, which now lay locked in the chest beside the window, but the thin, salt-stained account book Morozov had given him six weeks earlier. Every page inside it was balanced. Every timber receipt, every cooper’s tally, every deed of transfer for the Western fields bore two signatures: Dmitri’s cramped, hesitant hand and Morozov’s sharp, unforgiving stroke.

The woodcutters had arrived before dawn with ropes, iron wedges, and broad-headed axes. From the rise near the orchard, Katya watched them in silence, her shawl pulled tight across her shoulders. She had offered to walk down with him to sign the final release for the timber wagons, yet Dmitri had asked for ten minutes alone at the boundary stones.

"Look at them, Katya," he had murmured earlier on the porch, before the mist hid the lower pasture. "All winter I believed suffering was a currency I could hoard in my chest and spend in private. Now the trees are coming down to pay for barrels and roof-beams in Verkhnyaya, and there is nothing poetic in the sound of an axe."

A low crack echoed across the wet grass. One of the oldest birches leaned, shuddered through its pale crown, and sank into the ferns with a heavy, wet sigh that seemed to draw the dampness out of the very earth. Dmitri did not turn away. For the first time since the night he had opened his father’s desk, he felt neither feverish exaltation nor the suffocating weight of self-accusation. There was only the chill of the wind on his cheeks, the smell of split sap, and the plain arithmetic of what remained.

At the wagon track, Ivan Fyodorovich Morozov waited beside the clerk from the cooperage. His coat was damp at the shoulders, his boots caked in grey clay, yet his posture held the quiet steadiness of a man who measured grace in cordwood and cleared debts.

"The third cart is loaded, Dmitri Ivanovich," Morozov said, extending a brass-nibbed pen. "Sorokin’s men will take the straight road before dusk. Once you sign the tally, the perpetual clause stands satisfied through the winter."

Dmitri took the pen. His fingers were stiff with cold, yet the line he drew across the paper did not tremble. Beside the entry for the Western fields, he wrote the final note in a clear hand: *Paid in full, without reserve.*

"And what remains of the estate?" the clerk asked cautiously, blotting the ink.

"The house," Dmitri answered, looking back up the slope where Katya stood waiting by the orchard gate, "the small orchard, and enough timber to mend our own roof before November. Nothing that cannot be tended with our own hands."

Morozov nodded once—no praise, no sermon, only a brief flicker of recognition in his pale eyes—and stowed the tally in his satchel. Within half an hour the carts creaked down the lane toward the river, their iron rims cutting clean tracks through the mud.

When Dmitri climbed back to the porch, Katya had already lit the samovar. Inside, the parlor looked larger than it had in years, stripped of the heavy velvet drapes and gilded frames they had sold in April, yet strangely brighter for the bare plaster and clean floorboards.

She poured two glasses of tea and set his father’s old leather ledger on the table between them—not as an idol of torment, nor as a secret to be hidden, but simply as a finished book.

"Is it done, Dmitri?" she asked softly.

Dmitri looked down at the worn binding, then out through the casement toward the open sky above the cleared ridge. Where the dense grove had once shut out the horizon, pale afternoon light now spilled unimpeded across the meadow.

"The debt is paid," he said, lifting his glass with quiet steadiness. "And the promise—well, Katya, perhaps a promise is never truly finished. It only stops being a chain and becomes the road we walk."

Outside, the mist lifted from the hills, and for the first time in memory, the Volkov house stood open to the morning sun.
