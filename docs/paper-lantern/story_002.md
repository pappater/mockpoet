# The Second Key on the Ring

Dr. Mikhailov had carried the small brass key on his ring for eleven years without remembering which drawer it opened. Every morning when he hung his coat in the vestibule, the key clicked against his house key and the heavy iron latch of the dispensary; every evening, when he emptied his pockets onto the bedside saucer beside his spectacles, the brass key lay there gleaming faintly, patient and useless.

In truth, he had asked his wife Elena about it twice during their first year in the provincial house. Once she had laughed and said it surely belonged to an old trunkseller in Kazan; the second time she had been sorting winter linens and merely glanced up to say, "Leave it on the ring, Alyosha. The moment you throw away an unknown key, a locked box turns up."

Now it was late October, the birches along the carriage road were bare, and the dispensary smelled permanently of camphor and wet wool. Elena had gone to visit her sister in Tver for ten days. The house felt unusually tall in her absence, as if the ceilings had risen an inch when no one was talking beneath them.

On the fourth evening, searching the study for a misplaced prescription ledger, Mikhailov moved the walnut writing desk away from the wainscoting and noticed a narrow cedar chest tucked behind the bookshelf—a plain, dusty box he had assumed held spare lamp chimneys. Its brass keyhole was no wider than a fingernail.

He sat on his heels for a long minute before reaching into his pocket. Part of him preferred the mystery intact; while the key remained unassigned, it could still open anything—an attic in another city, a forgotten garden gate, a youthful promise. Yet curiosity, in a quiet house, is stronger than prudence.

The key turned with a dry, effortless click.

Inside lay no letters, no hidden debts, no dramatics worthy of a Petersburg feuilleton. There were only four neatly tied bundles: his own medical school notebooks with margins filled in his awkward early handwriting, three pressed oak leaves from the summer they had walked from the river station in the rain, a receipt for a silver thimble Elena had bought before they married, and a small velvet pin cushion shaped like a pear.

On top rested a slip of paper in Elena's neat, upright script: *In case you ever clean behind the bookcase and wonder why I kept your awful handwriting.*

Mikhailov sat by the stove until the birch logs settled into white ash. When he emptied his pockets onto the bedside saucer that night, he did not take the brass key off the ring. He set the key down gently beside his watch, wondering how many ordinary things in his house had quietly been keeping him safe all along.
